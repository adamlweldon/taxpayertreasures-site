How to buy a car, pickup or van from the government

Retired federal cars, pickups and vans are sold to the public on GSA Auctions, with free registration and no buyer's premium. How bidding, payment, pickup and the SF-97 title paperwork work, what to check first and what they sell for.

By Taxpayer Treasures · Published October 28, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026 · 9 min read

Every year federal, state and local agencies retire thousands of sedans, SUVs, pickups and vans, and most of them are sold to the public at auction. You don't need a dealer's license, there's no membership fee, and on the federal GSA Auctions site there's no buyer's premium on top of your bid. What you give up is the safety net: no warranty, no test drive, and a short window to pay and haul the vehicle away. Here's how it works, start to finish.

The idea in one minute

You find a vehicle on a government auction site, look at the photos and the description (and, where offered, see it in person), and bid online. If you win, you pay within a few days, collect the vehicle yourself by a deadline, and receive a government form called the SF-97 instead of a normal title. You take that form to your state's motor vehicle office, which issues a title and registration in your name.

There are two main federal channels, and they work a little differently:

  • GSA Auctions sells used federal property of all kinds online, vehicles included. All items are used, some heavily, and "GSA does not guarantee the condition of the sale items" (About GSA Auctions). This is where the bids in our records come from.
  • GSA Fleet Vehicle Sales sells vehicles from GSA's own leased fleet, typically after 3 to 5 years in service, through online and live auctions held at auction houses. GSA expects about 35,000 vehicles a year, mostly between April and September (GSA Fleet: About us).

States, counties, cities and school districts sell their own vehicles too, often on marketplaces such as GovDeals, where sedans and pickups regularly come up and are sold as-is with no warranty (Newsweek, February 2025). Their rules are set by each seller, so read each listing.

What comes up for sale

  • Sedans and SUVs. Agency cars such as Ford Fusions and Explorers and Jeep Grand Cherokees. GSA says its own fleet vehicles usually have one previous owner and come with most popular options (GSA Fleet: About us).
  • Police interceptors. Retired patrol sedans and SUVs. They usually have cloth front seats, vinyl rear seats, and wires and holes where lights, radios and partitions were mounted (Yahoo Autos).
  • Pickups. From half-ton trucks like the Ford F150 and Chevrolet Silverado to heavier F350s. Pickups are the most common vehicle in our records.
  • Vans. Passenger vans and cargo vans, such as Ford E-series.
  • Utility and service trucks. Pickups with service bodies, toolboxes or lift gates. Larger trucks over 10,000 pounds may need a different trip permit to drive home (see below).

How a GSA Auctions sale works

1. Register. Registration is free. You sign up as an individual or a company, and GSA checks your identity through Experian. A credit card can help with verification but isn't charged. One account per Social Security number (GSA Auctions FAQ). Register as a company only if you want the SF-97 in the company's name: the name on the SF-97 can't be changed after the award (GSA Auctions FAQ).

2. Bid. Each bid must be at least the current high bid plus the bid increment. Bids can't be cancelled. Ties go to whoever bid first, and GSA advises against waiting until the final seconds (GSA Auctions FAQ). Some sale offices require a bid deposit; the item description says so.

3. Pay. GSA Auctions takes credit cards, debit cards with a card-network logo, and wire transfers. There's a $24,999.99 limit per card, and no more than two cards per transaction, so a pricier truck may need a wire. Debit cards with spending limits or PINs aren't accepted (GSA Auctions FAQ). The payment deadline is in the sale's terms and your award email, so read it before you bid. For comparison, GSA Fleet's own internet vehicle sales give 2 business days to pay (GSA Fleet Internet Terms).

4. Remove it. You pick up the vehicle yourself, or send someone with a signed letter of authorization, their photo ID and the signed Purchaser's Receipt. GSA does not ship (GSA Auctions FAQ). GSA Fleet's internet sales give 10 calendar days to remove a vehicle and grant no extensions (GSA Fleet Internet Terms). Under the general federal sale terms, title passes to you when removal is complete (SF 114C).

5. As-is, where-is. Federal property is sold "as is" and "where is", with no warranty of quality or fitness for use (SF 114C). GSA isn't responsible for defects found after the award (About GSA Auctions). Some GSA Fleet vehicles may still have a transferable manufacturer warranty; check with the manufacturer (GSA Fleet: About us).

Missing a deadline is expensive. On GSA Auctions, if you fail to pay or remove, you owe liquidated damages: the full award amount for awards under $325, $325 for awards from $325 to $100,000, and 5% above that. Unpaid amounts go to the Treasury for collection, and you can lose bidding rights until it's settled (GSA Auctions FAQ).

The title paperwork: the SF-97

Instead of a state title, you get the SF-97, the U.S. Government Certificate to Obtain Title to a Vehicle (GSA Forms Library). Federal sale terms say an SF-97 is provided for vehicles that need state licensing unless the listing says otherwise (SF 114C). GSA Fleet's terms are clear on one point that catches people out: "The SF-97-1 is not a motor vehicle registration." It is evidence of ownership that lets your state issue a title (GSA Fleet Internet Terms). Vehicles sold as salvage get a branded SF-97, and a new one isn't issued after repairs. Replacing a lost SF-97 takes a written request and may carry a fee (GSA Fleet Internet Terms).

What your DMV wants varies by state. Florida is a good example of what to expect: it treats the SF-97 like a title, and asks for the SF-97, its title application form, a VIN verification if the vehicle isn't already registered in Florida, sales tax, plate information and title fees (Florida HSMV TL-14). Other states may require a safety or emissions inspection. GSA Fleet's terms also say buyers must meet EPA and NHTSA requirements (GSA Fleet Internet Terms). Call or check your own DMV's website before you bid, not after.

What to check before you bid

  • Inspection days. Bidders are urged to inspect before bidding, at the times and places in the listing (SF 114C). At GSA Fleet live auctions you can usually start a vehicle and check the engine, A/C and heater, but not drive it (iSeeCars). If you can't go, read every word of the description and study every photo.
  • Mileage. The odometer reading is in the listing. Fleet cars are often young, but a few years of agency driving can still mean a lot of miles.
  • Idle hours on police vehicles. Patrol cars spend much of their lives running while parked. A study of police cars in Ottawa found the average police vehicle idles up to 6.7 hours of every 10-hour shift (Guide Auto Web, 2012). That wear doesn't show on the odometer, so a low-mileage interceptor can be more worn than it looks.
  • Holes and wiring. Removed light bars, radios, consoles and push bars leave holes and cut wires (Yahoo Autos). Look at the roof, dashboard, pillars and front bumper in the photos, and budget for patching.
  • "Does not start" and "needs a jump." Vehicles can sit for weeks or months before sale, and many listings note they need a boost. Bring a jump pack if you inspect (Yahoo Autos). Take "does not start" literally: plan to tow it.
  • Keys and known problems. Check the listing says keys are included. GSA Fleet lists known problems in the sale information, but doesn't vouch for the condition of other agencies' vehicles (GSA Fleet: About us).
  • Paint. Black-and-white police cars tend to sell for less than all-black or single-color ones (Yahoo Autos).

From winning bid to driving home

  1. Pay by the deadline in your award email.
  2. Get insurance before you drive it. Your insurer will want the VIN, which is in the listing. Trip permits in Oregon and Virginia both ask for insurance details (Oregon DMV; Virginia DMV).
  3. Arrange a way to move it legally. Plates don't come with government vehicles. Some states sell short trip permits: Oregon's light vehicle trip permit is $35 for 21 days, and its heavy vehicle permit (over 10,000 pounds) is $43 for 10 days (Oregon DMV). Virginia has a $5 prospective purchaser's trip permit, good for three days from the purchase date (Virginia DMV). Rules and prices vary by state, and some buyers simply trailer or tow the vehicle home.
  4. Pick it up with your Purchaser's Receipt and ID, before the removal deadline.
  5. Title and register it at your DMV with the SF-97.
  6. Pay sales or use tax. GSA Auctions doesn't add sales tax, but your state may charge it when you title the vehicle (GSA Auctions FAQ). Rates vary by state.

Who this suits

A good fit if you're comfortable judging a vehicle from photos and a description, can pay and collect within days, and have a mechanic, a trailer or a friend with a truck. It suits work-truck shoppers and second-car buyers who would rather do their own checking than pay a dealer to do it.

Not a fit if you need financing, a warranty, a test drive or a car you can drive away the same afternoon with no paperwork. A used-car dealer costs more, but carries less risk.
