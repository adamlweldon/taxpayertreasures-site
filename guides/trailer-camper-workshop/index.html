Turn a government surplus trailer into a camper or mobile workshop

Cargo, utility and military trailers sell at government auctions, often for a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. Here's what comes up, the paperwork and towing catches, how a conversion goes and what it costs.

By Taxpayer Treasures · Published October 28, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026 · 9 min read

A trailer is one of the easiest government surplus buys to turn into something useful. Agencies sell enclosed cargo trailers, open utility trailers, equipment trailers and ex-military cargo trailers, and the empty box or bed is a blank space you can make into a small camper, a tool room on wheels, or both. It's a far smaller project than restoring a truck. It still has catches: titles, hitches, brakes, old tires, and one category of trailer with a real health history behind it. Here's how to work through them.

The idea in one minute

You buy a sound trailer, make it legal and safe to tow, then build the inside. For a camper that means insulation, ventilation, a window or two, a bed and a small power system. For a workshop it means shelving, a workbench, lighting and power. Many people get a cheap military trailer and keep it simple: a rooftop tent or a canvas cover over a box that already carries gear.

It has been done:

  • An M101 from a government auction. A Jeep owner on JK-Forum won an M101 cargo trailer at a government surplus auction in French Camp, California, with a winning bid of $585, about $675 after tax and a 10% buyer's fee. He had to fill in an end-use form and wait about 60 days for approval before pickup, then weighed keeping the pintle hitch, rewiring the lights and adding a fold-over canvas shell (JK-Forum).
  • A used cargo trailer turned into a tiny camper. The couple behind the DIY Cargo Trailer YouTube channel bought a used 18-foot tandem-axle cargo trailer for $5,000 and spent about $15,000 converting it, with a shower, toilet, kitchen, water tanks, a bed that lowers from the ceiling and a heater/AC on the tongue (Motor1, December 2022).
  • A budget conversion. A contractor bought a used 6x14 enclosed trailer with a rough camper conversion already in it for $4,300, redid the electrical and plumbing, and finished at about $10,000 to $10,500 all-in, including two 200 amp-hour lithium batteries, a 2,000-watt inverter and 300 watts of solar (RV Inspiration, 2023).
  • A detailed insulation write-up. A builder on the Tiny Teardrop Trailer forum documented a 6x12 cargo conversion: about 1-3/4 inches of foam in the walls over the factory metal studs, foam glued between the floor cross members, framed window openings and a hinged rooftop solar rack (TNTTT, 2021-22).

What actually comes up for sale

Look for cargo trailer, enclosed trailer, utility trailer, equipment trailer and military model numbers in lot titles. The usual kinds:

  • Enclosed cargo trailers. The most common starting point for a camper or workshop: walls, roof and doors are already there.
  • Open utility and equipment trailers. Good for a tool platform, a rooftop-tent base or a flatbed build. Heavy equipment trailers (one Kalyn Lo-Boy in our records) are a different class of machine.
  • Military cargo trailers. The best-known family:
  • M101 (3/4-ton). A steel box on a single axle with a lunette ring. For the M101A2, 4 Wheels and a Motor gives 2,250 lb of payload on the highway and 1,500 lb off road, with tow speed limited to 15 mph cross-country.
  • M105. Bigger: a maximum payload of 3,000 lb, a 24-volt lighting system, and limits of 50 mph on highways and 30 mph cross-country (OpenVMS Hobbyist). The M105A3 weighs about 2,700 lb empty and 5,700 lb loaded, and measures 177.5 x 94 x 97 inches (M105A3 transport data).
  • M1102 (the Humvee trailer). The Army manual lists a 1,460 lb curb weight, 2,740 lb payload, 4,200 lb maximum gross weight, 37x12.50R16.5 tires, hydraulic surge brakes with breakaway actuation, a 24-volt DC lighting system and an overall size of 132 x 85.6 x 52.5 inches (TM, May 2002).
  • Travel trailers. Ready-made campers, sometimes ex-disaster housing. Read the next section before you bid on one.

Our records. We tracked 35 closed trailer lots with bids on GSA Auctions between October 13 and 23, 2026 (that count includes some boats sold with trailers). The median last bid we saw was $1,020; the middle half went for between $325 and $5,121, and the full range ran from $20 to $37,800. Three lots titled "Travel Trailer" in Alabama closed on October 13 at $37,800 (20 bidders), $11,325 and $9,114, and the Kalyn Lo-Boy closed at $14,580 in Oregon. These are the last bids we saw, not official sale prices.

A warning about ex-FEMA travel trailers

After Hurricane Katrina, FEMA housed families in tens of thousands of trailers, and many had high formaldehyde levels. The CDC tested 519 occupied units in Louisiana and Mississippi between December 2007 and January 2008. The average (geometric mean) was 77 ppb overall and 81 ppb in travel trailers, against about 17 ppb in a 2005 study of US homes. 42% of travel trailers were at or above 100 ppb, the level at which the CDC says health effects have been described in sensitive people. Levels went up with heat and humidity, and the CDC said winter testing probably understated summer exposure (CDC final report).

Those trailers were later sold off. When a court ban on sales expired in 2010, GSA auctioned the remaining units. Buyers had to agree not to resell them as housing, and the trailers carried stickers saying they were not for human habitation, only storage or recreation. High Country News reported that the stickers often disappeared and that ex-FEMA units were resold as homes. A formaldehyde test kit cost about $35 at the time (High Country News; NPR).

What that means for you: if a travel trailer listing mentions FEMA, disaster housing or a habitation restriction, read every condition in it and plan for its use. Heat, humidity and closed windows all pushed levels up in the CDC study, so a hot, closed-up trailer is the worst case. Test before you sleep in any older trailer, and ventilate. The CDC's advice to residents was to open windows and doors, keep indoor temperatures as low as comfortable and not smoke inside.

Questions to answer before you bid

1. Can you tow it, and does the hitch match?

Find your tow vehicle's towing capacity in its owner's manual and compare it with the trailer's loaded weight, not its empty weight. A built-out camper is heavier than the empty box. Military trailers use a lunette ring, a steel eye that needs a pintle hook on the tow vehicle, not a ball. The JK-Forum buyer found a pintle set for about $100 at Tractor Supply; others swap the lunette for a coupler (Expedition Portal).

2. What will the title paperwork be?

Under GSA's general sale terms, motor-drawn equipment that needs state licensing comes with a Standard Form 97, the government certificate used to get a title, unless the listing says otherwise (SF 114C). GSA Fleet's terms say the SF 97 is evidence of title only, not a registration, and doesn't waive fees or taxes; vehicles sold as salvage get a branded one (GSA Fleet terms). Some lots don't come with one, so check the listing.

State rules vary a lot. Texas requires all non-farm trailers used on public roads to be registered and manufactured trailers over 4,000 lb gross to be titled. Rhode Island titles trailers with a GVWR of 3,001 lb or more and requires a safety inspection at 1,000 lb and up. South Dakota puts homemade trailers over 3,000 lb through a rebuilt-vehicle inspection. Check your own state's DMV page before you bid. The Maryland M101A2 owner paid $235 in title, temporary tag and paperwork fees, plus sales tax and inspection (4 Wheels and a Motor).

3. Will it need brakes?

The weight at which a state requires trailer brakes ranges from over 1,000 lb in North Carolina to over 4,500 lb in Texas; the most common figure is about 3,000 lb (RVIA chart, 2017; check current law in your state). Many small military trailers have only parking brakes. The M101A1 at Expedition Portal had no surge brakes, and posters there warned that the brakes are a common failure point on trailers that sit (Expedition Portal).

4. How old are the tires and bearings?

Surplus trailers often sit for years. The last four digits of a tire's DOT code give the week and year it was made (0308 means the third week of 2008), and the code may be on only one side. NHTSA says you can't detect aging just by looking, and that some manufacturers recommend replacing tires six to 10 years old regardless of tread (NHTSA). Dexter, a major axle maker, says bearings should be lubricated every 12 months or 12,000 miles (Dexter). On an unknown trailer, assume both need attention before a long tow.

The build, step by step

  1. Make it roadworthy first. Tires, bearings, brakes, lights and hitch, then title and plates. Nothing inside matters if it can't get home legally.
  2. Sort the lights. Military trailers run 24-volt lighting, and civilian tow vehicles supply 12 volts. Builders either rewire with a standard flat connector and LED lights, as the JK-Forum buyer planned, or use magnetic tow lights to start (JK-Forum; Expedition Portal).
  3. Strip, clean and seal. Find and fix roof and door leaks before you cover the walls.
  4. Insulate. Rigid foam between the wall studs and floor members, as in the TNTTT build, then a plywood or paneling inner wall.
  5. Ventilate and add windows. Even more important in a camper: moisture builds up quickly in a small space, and in older trailers ventilation also matters for formaldehyde. Frame each opening before cutting in a window or roof vent.
  6. Add power. For a camper, a battery, inverter and solar, like the RV Inspiration build's 400 amp-hours of lithium, 2,000-watt inverter and 300 watts of solar. For a workshop, a shore-power inlet for tools and good LED lighting. Get an electrician to check anything that connects to mains power.
  7. Fit it out. A bed platform with storage underneath for a camper; shelving bolted to the frame, a workbench and tie-downs for a workshop. Weigh it when you're done and compare with the trailer's rated gross weight.

Who should try this

A good fit if you have a tow vehicle with capacity to spare, somewhere to park and work, and basic carpentry and wiring skills. A small trailer is a manageable first build, and a workshop can be done in weekends.

Not a fit if you want a finished camper next month, or can't tow what the trailer will weigh when it's built. A used camper built for the job may cost less than your time.
