Backup power for your home from a government surplus generator

Can a used military or government generator back up your house? Often, if you pick the right size and have an electrician connect it safely. What comes up at auction, what it sells for, what to check and the safety rules.

By Taxpayer Treasures · Published October 26, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026 · 9 min read

A new whole-house standby generator costs about $5,156 installed on average, according to HomeAdvisor's June 2026 figures, and the typical range runs from $1,512 to $8,803. Government auctions sell used generators for a lot less, including the diesel "tactical quiet" sets the military has used for decades. Some buyers end up with a tough, efficient backup for their house, farm or shop. Others end up with a 1,200-pound machine with no batteries, drained fluids and a missing part nobody makes any more. Here's how to tell the difference, and how to hook one up without hurting anyone.

The idea in one minute

You buy a surplus generator that's the right size for the loads you care about, get it running, set it on a pad outside, and have a licensed electrician connect it to your panel through a transfer switch or interlock. When the power goes out, you start it, flip the switch, and run the circuits you chose.

Owners have done it:

  • An MEP-803A backing up a whole house. A Steel Soldiers forum member set up a military 10 kW MEP-803A with a discounted, service-rated Generac automatic transfer switch and a controller made for MEP sets. He chose to start the generator by hand because he didn't want it fully automatic while nobody was home. He also needed an electrician, and getting on one's schedule could take weeks (Steel Soldiers).
  • A cheaper manual setup. In the same thread, another owner installed a 200-amp panel with a manual interlock kit (about $350), paid a $25 county inspection fee and spent about $450 on wire, armored conduit and a disconnect. His generator sits on a concrete pad about 30 feet from the house (Steel Soldiers).
  • A $1,600 gamble. Another member bought an MEP-803A for $1,600 that was sold "not operationally checked" because its fluids had been drained. Its batteries were listed as missing, and parts of the trailer looked to be gone. Other members said drained fluids and pulled batteries are normal for surplus sets (Steel Soldiers).

What actually comes up for sale

Look for generator, genset, generator set and MEP- in auction titles. In our records of 21 closed GSA generator lots with bids (October 13 to 23, 2026), the median last bid we saw was $1,206, the middle half went for $519 to $1,800, and the range was $100 to $14,881. Many of these were big industrial or towable units, not home-sized ones. Expect a few kinds of lot:

  • Military tactical quiet generators. These are the MEP-800 series. The MEP-831A puts out 3 kW at 120/240 V, weighs 334 pounds with fluids and burns about 0.5 gallon an hour. The MEP-802A is 5 kW and weighs 888 pounds with fluids. The MEP-803A is 10 kW, weighs 1,242 pounds with fluids and burns about 0.99 gallon an hour from a 9-gallon tank, so a full tank lasts roughly nine hours at that average rate. All of them run on diesel or JP-8 (Marine Corps Systems Command; Coleman's). Their newer replacement, the AMMPS MEP-1040 (10 kW, 1,060 pounds with fluids), shows up too.
  • Trailer-mounted towables. These are job-site and military generators on their own trailers. In our records, the top lot was a Magnum 130 generator and trailer at $14,881 (California, 4 bidders), and a Doosan G70 with trailer went for $5,010 (California).
  • Big standby and industrial units. These came out of buildings and include sets like a DMI 100 kW at $1,800 (Arkansas, 8 bidders) and a Kohler 6063MK35 engine generator at $5,985 (Massachusetts, 7 bidders). A 100 kW set is many times what a house needs. Generac puts a typical 2,000-square-foot whole-home system at about 20 kW. Big units also often come wired for commercial three-phase power.
  • Portable generators. These are smaller gasoline units, sometimes sold in lots of several.

Five questions to answer before you bid

1. How much power do you actually need?

Generac's rule of thumb is to add up the running watts of everything you want on, then add the biggest single starting surge. Motors are what catch people out: starting a motor can take two to three times its running power. A 3-ton central air conditioner runs on about 3.5 to 5 kW but can need 15 to 18 kW to start. Generac suggests about 10 kW for essentials in a roughly 1,550-square-foot home (fridge, freezer, lights, sump pump, internet) and 14 to 18 kW if you add central air or a well pump. Read the nameplates on your well pump, fridge and furnace before you set a budget (Generac).

A 10 kW MEP-803A fits the essentials-only case. A 3 kW MEP-831A will run a fridge and some lights but not much else.

2. Will its voltage and phase work with your house?

US homes use single-phase power (Purdue Extension). Many military sets can be switched between modes. Coleman's lists the MEP-802A as single- and three-phase at 120/208/240 V, and owners describe a reconnection switch or box that sets single or three phase (TractorByNet). An owner on the same forum noted that a switch doesn't guarantee full rated output in every mode. Industrial units built for three-phase only are another matter. Treat any of this as a question for your electrician before you bid, not after.

3. Is it complete, and how many hours are on it?

On Steel Soldiers, experienced owners say "complete" is the key word. Parts that rarely fail can be the hardest to find, and units are sometimes stripped before sale. One member reported 2025 average sale prices of about $1,381 for MEP-802s (average 662 hours) and $1,888 for MEP-803s (average 1,682 hours), with location affecting price most. He also put auction premiums and taxes at roughly 18% (Steel Soldiers). Look for the hour meter in the photos, and look for missing batteries, cut wires, mouse nests and a missing voltage regulator.

4. Can you move it?

Skid-mounted sets need a forklift or loader at both ends. Trailer-mounted military sets usually have a lunette ring for a pintle hook. Owners say the ring sits 32 to 34 inches off the ground, so a pickup usually needs a raised pintle mount. They say the stock safety chains are too short for a receiver hitch and the trailer lights use a 12-pin military plug. One owner also described his state's title process for the trailer. Weight sits toward the control end, so load carefully (Steel Soldiers). Government sales are as-is, and you handle removal.

5. What fuel can you store?

Military sets burn diesel or JP-8. Civilian units may run on gasoline, natural gas or propane, and the listing should say which. OSHA's advice is to store fuel in vented containers made and labeled for that fuel, away from the generator and other heat sources, and never in your home. Let the generator cool before refueling, because spilled fuel can ignite on hot engine parts (OSHA). Local rules on how much fuel you can store vary, so check yours.

The project, step by step

  1. Get it home and inspect it before cranking. Owners recommend the before-operation checks in the Army technical manual and a full look for missing parts and pests. Turn the fan by hand. Replace oil, coolant, filters, fuel and batteries. Fix fuel leaks, which are common, and do the known modifications before the first start. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and know how to block the air intake if a diesel runs away (Steel Soldiers).
  2. Choose a spot outside. CPSC says to run generators outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, never on a porch or in a carport, with the exhaust pointed away from buildings. CDC says to keep generators at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, and never to run one in a home, garage or carport. OSHA adds 3 to 4 feet of clear space on all sides and above, and warns that opening windows or doors doesn't stop CO from building up in enclosed spaces.
  3. Check local rules. Permits, setbacks and noise limits vary by town and state. One example: in 2013, Scarsdale, New York capped standby generators at 70 decibels measured 23 feet away and set hours for test runs (Scarsdale10583). Military tactical quiet sets are rated at 70 dBA at 7 meters (Marine Corps Systems Command). HomeAdvisor puts permits at $50 to $200 each.
  4. Have a licensed electrician connect it. This is the step you can't skip. Plugging a generator into house wiring without a transfer switch, for example through a dryer outlet, sends power backward through your panel onto the utility lines. That can electrocute line workers who think the lines are dead, and it can cause fires when grid power comes back. Generac notes it's illegal in many places, and OSHA says to connect a generator to a building only through a transfer switch a qualified electrician installed. A transfer switch or an interlock (a bracket that stops the main breaker and the generator breaker from both being on) makes power flow only one way (Consumer Reports).
  5. Install CO alarms. CPSC recommends battery or battery-backup CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, ideally interconnected, tested monthly. CPSC estimates about 100 people die each year in the US from CO from portable generators.
  6. Write the steps down and practice. One owner's advice was to write step-by-step instructions for everyone in the house. Practicing helped in a real outage (Steel Soldiers).

Who should try this

A good fit if you're comfortable with diesel engines and wiring diagrams, have a way to move 1,000-plus pounds, and want a durable backup for a house, farm or shop. A known runner is the safest buy if you can't troubleshoot electrical faults yourself.

Not a fit if you want hands-off, automatic backup next week, or if your loads need more than about 10 kW. A new standby unit with a warranty may suit you better. And skip industrial sets sized for a whole building unless you have a building to power.
