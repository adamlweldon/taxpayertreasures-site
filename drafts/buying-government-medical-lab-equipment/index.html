How to buy medical and lab equipment at government auctions

Federal hospitals and labs sell microscopes, centrifuges, freezers, dental chairs, ultrasound and much more on GSA Auctions. Who can buy what, the Medical Device Statement of Intent, power and software catches, X-ray rules, pickup and what it sells for.

By Taxpayer Treasures · Updated October 28, 2026 · 9 min read

Federal hospitals, clinics and research labs replace equipment all the time, and what they don't pass on to other agencies or donate ends up for sale to the public. On any given day there are hundreds of lots: microscopes, centrifuges, incubators, lab freezers, autoclaves, exam tables, dental chairs, hospital beds, ultrasound systems and boxes of supplies. Some go for less than lunch. Others draw serious bidding. But medical equipment comes with rules most auction lots don't have, and many lots can only go to a licensed buyer. Here's what to know before you bid.

The idea in one minute

When a federal agency no longer needs something, it reports it to GSA. GSA first offers it to other federal agencies, then to eligible groups such as state and local agencies and certain nonprofits through each state's surplus property agency. Whatever nobody claims is sold to the public on GSA Auctions (GSA Blog). So by the time a microscope or a centrifuge reaches the auction site, it has already been passed over by the government.

Registering and bidding on GSA Auctions is free, and there's no buyer's premium (GSA Auctions FAQ). Everything is sold "as is" and "where is", with no warranty (SF 114C), and you collect it yourself.

In our records, the agencies selling lab and medical lots include the Department of Veterans Affairs, Health and Human Services, the CDC, USDA's Agricultural Research Service, the EPA and the U.S. Geological Survey.

What comes up for sale

  • Lab instruments. Microscopes, centrifuges, incubators, spectrometers, analyzers, fume hoods and biological safety cabinets.
  • Cold storage. Lab refrigerators and freezers, including ultra-low freezers that run at -80°C.
  • Clinic and hospital equipment. Exam tables, hospital beds, stretcher-chairs, infusion and feeding pumps, ventilators, defibrillators, ultrasound systems and ophthalmology instruments.
  • Dental equipment. Chairs, imaging sensors and sometimes dental X-ray units.
  • Supplies. Mixed lots of surgical instruments, medical supplies, test kits and lab consumables. Some are past their expiry date, and listings sometimes say so.

The open lab and medical lots are listed further down this page and update every day.

Many lots are for licensed buyers only

This is the big difference from other auction categories. Many medical devices are prescription devices. Under 21 CFR 801.109, a prescription device is one that isn't safe to use without supervision by a licensed practitioner. Its labeling must carry the caution "Federal law restricts this device to sale by or on the order of a" physician, dentist or other licensed practitioner (FDA).

GSA Auctions handles this through a form. Of the 367 open lab and medical lots we were tracking on October 28, 2026, 193 required the winning bidder to sign a Medical Device Statement of Intent before GSA would accept payment or make the award. Some listings go further. Lots of ventilators and electric hospital beds, for example, said they were controlled devices that could only be sold to a licensed physician or an authorized business. Others said the form was needed to check professional use or a license. One lot of ophthalmoscopes said buyers were responsible for checking FDA compliance before use. A lot of surgical instruments said the buyer had to handle clinical validation and sterilization before any use on people.

What this means for you:

  • Read the whole description before you bid. If it mentions a Statement of Intent or a license, assume you'll be asked to prove who you are and what the equipment is for. If you can't, don't bid. A won auction you can't complete can cost you liquidated damages (see the cost table).
  • Plain lab gear is usually simpler. A benchtop centrifuge, a microscope or a lab fridge is not the same as a ventilator. Still, check every listing; the rules are set lot by lot.
  • Reselling is regulated too. Other medical marketplaces handle this the same way. DOTmed's standard auction terms, for example, say prescription-use devices may be sold only as parts or for servicing to qualified buyers, not for use on patients, and expired items only for research, veterinary or educational use (DOTmed).
  • Fixing and rebuilding has its own rules. In May 2024, the FDA finalized guidance on where ordinary servicing ends and remanufacturing begins. Work that significantly changes a device's performance, safety or intended use can count as remanufacturing, which brings manufacturer-level obligations (MedTech Dive). If your plan is to refurbish devices for clinical use, talk to someone who knows FDA rules first.

Special cases

X-ray machines

Dental and medical X-ray units are regulated by the state, not just the FDA, and the rules differ by state:

  • Florida: Anyone who acquires a radiation machine must apply to register it within 30 days of acquiring it and before using it. The seller or transferor must notify the state within 15 days (Florida Administrative Code 64E-5.511).
  • Arizona: You must register a nonexempt radiation machine within 30 days after installing it (Arizona R9-7-202). Under Arizona law, only a licensed practitioner, or someone certified and working at a practitioner's direction, may use ionizing radiation on a person (Maven Imaging).

Check your own state's radiation control program before you bid on anything that makes X-rays.

Tablet presses and capsule fillers

The DEA regulates tableting and encapsulating machines: machines that press powder into tablets or fill capsules, whether manual or automatic. Every sale, import or export of one is a "regulated transaction." It must be reported to the DEA, with the buyer's identity checked and records kept for two years (DEA). In 2024, eBay agreed to pay a $59 million civil penalty over unreported sales of these machines (FDA Law Blog). If a lot includes one, expect paperwork, and know that you take on the same duties if you ever sell it on.

Hazardous materials and decontamination

Labs are supposed to clean equipment before it leaves. At Stanford, for example, equipment going to surplus must be drained and cleaned of chemical and radiological residue, with biological material disinfected. Biosafety cabinets need a disinfection certificate, and the department must certify decontamination in writing. Sealed radioactive sources, such as those in some liquid scintillation counters, must be removed by health physics staff. Some ovens and furnaces can contain asbestos and can't be resold at all (Stanford EH&S). Federal listings vary in how much they tell you. A few lots in our records include chemicals such as formalin, with terms making the buyer responsible for handling, storage and disposal under all applicable laws. Ask the property custodian about decontamination before you bid on anything that touched blood, tissue, chemicals or radioactive material.

What to check before you bid

  • Power. Much lab equipment doesn't run on an ordinary outlet. Ultra-low freezers, for example, come in 115 V and 208 to 230 V versions (Thermo Fisher TSX). One 208/230 V model uses a NEMA 6-15 plug (American Laboratory Trading). Thermo Fisher calls for a dedicated, grounded circuit (Thermo Fisher 8930086V). Look for the voltage and plug on the nameplate in the photos, and price in an electrician if needed. A few listings in our records mention three-phase power, which most homes don't have.
  • Running costs. Thermo Fisher's own figures put a modern -80°C freezer at about 7.9 to 10.4 kWh a day. In its comparison, an older model used 31.9 kWh a day (Thermo Fisher TSX). That's roughly 11,600 kWh a year, every day the freezer runs.
  • Refrigerant. Newer lab freezers often use hydrocarbon refrigerants such as R290 (propane) and R170 (Thermo Fisher 8930086V). These carry an A3 safety rating, meaning they're flammable, and servicing them calls for technicians trained in hydrocarbons (ACHR News).
  • Age and service history. Lab Manager advises asking for service history and calibration logs. A unit that was under a manufacturer service contract may have a calibration history the maker can confirm by serial number. Cold storage is best bought no more than seven to eight years old, since that's about how long compressors typically last (Lab Manager). Many federal listings give the acquisition date and warranty expiry; use them.
  • Computers, software and hard drives. Federal agencies must sanitize storage media before equipment is reused, recycled or disposed of (EPA). For you, that can mean a wiped or missing hard drive. One field analyzer in our records had its hard drive removed and needed a new one plus software before use. Instruments that depend on a PC, a license or a manufacturer login may need help from the maker to run again, and that support isn't guaranteed.
  • "Untested" and "salvage." Listings often say functionality hasn't been verified since the item was taken out of service. Take that literally. Of the open lab and medical lots we tracked, 13 were listed in salvage condition.
  • Weight and size. An ultra-low freezer's shipping weight runs from about 665 to 894 pounds, and the larger models need a doorway at least 34.5 inches wide (Thermo Fisher TSX). Autoclaves, dental chairs and hospital beds are heavy too. Measure your doors before you bid.

Inspection, payment and pickup

Inspect if you can. Federal sale terms urge bidders to inspect before bidding (SF 114C). Of the 367 open lots we tracked, 322 said inspection and removal were by confirmed appointment only, and that the agency would provide no help with packing, loading or shipping. Some sites are access-controlled: a couple of listings required everyone to be a U.S. citizen with a REAL ID, and noted that no forklift was available.

Pay on time. GSA Auctions takes credit cards, debit cards with a card-network logo and wire transfers. There's a $24,999.99 limit per card, with up to two cards per transaction (GSA Auctions FAQ). If a lot needs a Statement of Intent, plan for it to be checked before payment goes through.

Collect it yourself. GSA doesn't ship. You or an agent with a letter of authorization, the purchaser's receipt and photo ID must pick it up (GSA Auctions FAQ). Bring straps, moving blankets, a hand truck or pallet jack, and enough people. Plan for liftgate trucking on anything heavy.

Who this suits

A good fit if you run or supply a small lab, school, clinic, veterinary practice or makerspace, or you're a hobbyist who wants a microscope, centrifuge or incubator. Also if you're a licensed practitioner or an authorized business, and if you service this kind of equipment. You'll need to read listings closely, handle heavy items and do your own testing.

Not a fit if you need equipment certified, calibrated and ready for patient use, with installation and a warranty. A dealer who refurbishes and supports equipment costs more but takes that work off your hands. And if a lot is restricted to licensed buyers and you aren't one, skip it.
